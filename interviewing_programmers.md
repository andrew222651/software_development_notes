# Interviewing Programmers

Predictably, Robin Hanson [suggests](https://www.overcomingbias.com/2022/05/new-hire-prediction-markets.html)
using prediction markets to evaluate candidates.
Variants to create a separating equilibrium include candidates paying an application fee (illegal), or offering free trials
or money-back guarantees.
Naturally there may be good reasons why such mechanisms aren't currently common.

Companies generally reject candidates outright rather than simply offering what
they think they're worth. I don't fully understand this.

offering feedback to applicants costs you little but may be very valuable to
them


### Sourcing

GitHub has "available for hire" flag on profiles but workable API is not
available and they got rid of their own jobs platform. So companies can't seek
out candidates, candidates must seek out the company. To determine where to advertise,
just ask you current employees where they'd look for a job.


### Criteria

can they code?
* work experience ("best predictor of future performance")
* past open source contributions. if they don't have any, give them an [open
  source issue](https://goodfirstissue.dev/) to resolve (maybe even pay them for it)
  * this includes writing: web Q&A contributions or expository articles about technology (again, invite
    them to write something new if they don't have anything already)
* coding tests
  * an idea for a short but realistic test: code review a PR
  * ideally use previous results from centralized testing service like
    [Otherbranch](https://www.otherbranch.com/),
    training course (e.g. college transcript), or coding contests
  * you can try to look up what the interview processes were at the candidate's
    previous companies. if the candidate has passed similar tests before you may
    not need to do much additional testing.

are they socially skilled? how's their English?


#### are they smart?

apparently this is a good predictor of career success

measuring intelligence without tests:
* intelligence ~= problem solving ability.
* problems are solved either with a previous preprocessing step or not.
* preprocessing is integrating knowledge to apply later, i.e. learning, so
  we ask questions about their skills that use this preprocessing
* without preprocessing: problems are discrete and memorable. so we ask about
  problems solved in the past.
  * the earlier and more frequent the better
  * some are direct from resumes: scientific publications (especially
    single-author)
* "we can easily judge other people's intelligence if we hear them say [...]
  insightful things" -Geoffrey Miller
* "it's very easy [to identify the people who aren't smart]. you you talk to
  them and you can rapidly surface whether they're smart or not. You know, what
  was your master's thesis? What did you do? how did it exactly work? Well, how
  did you deal with error conditions? how many processes did you have? Why
  didn't you use threads? I only ask them deep technical
  questions." -Stonebraker


### In the interview

Keep it short. Resumes already contain a lot of information.
Interviews can verify resume facts and address remaining uncertainty.

interview goals to keep in mind
* make them feel good about themselves
* make them want to work there
* try to get to know the person rather than interrogate them

There's [some evidence](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/04/08/opinion/sunday/the-utter-uselessness-of-job-interviews.html)
that doing anything in an interview other than verifying what's on the
candidate's resume is at best a waste of time.
(Hanson [says](https://www.overcomingbias.com/2014/07/firm-inefficiency.html#:~:text=too%20many%20people.-,Too%20Many%20Interviews,-%E2%80%93%20It%20is%20hard)
interviews are used to determine if the candidate would align with the
interviewer's coalition.)
Given this, I think it's still good to keep the style of interviews as natural
and conversational as possible, which helps the interviewee feel comfortable
(improving the quality of the interview and making the candidate feel good about
the experience).
